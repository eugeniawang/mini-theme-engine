# The five stages of customer awareness

This note is the rulebook for stage 2. It says what each awareness stage means, which words in a buyer's own speech place them there, and how to tell two neighbouring stages apart.

## Where the framework comes from

Eugene Schwartz set it out in *Breakthrough Advertising* (1966). His point: before you write a headline, ask how much your prospect already knows about their desire, about the ways to satisfy it, and about your product. The less they know, the more ground the message has to cover before it can make an offer.

The summaries checked here describe the stages in sentences and list them from most aware to least aware, which suggests the book does the same; this note has not confirmed that against the book. The short labels used below (unaware, problem aware, solution aware, product aware, most aware) are the common names. Joanna Wiebe of Copyhackers calls the second stage "pain aware". The four secondary sources listed below agree on what each stage means.

## The five stages

Ordered from least to most aware, because that is the order a buyer moves in.

| Stage | Definition | What the buyer says (signals) |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Unaware | The buyer describes a cost or a symptom but does not treat it as a problem to fix. Summaries of Schwartz put it as: they do not recognize the desire, or will not admit it. | Treats it as normal or acceptable: "everyone in my trade deals with that", "it comes with the territory", "I don't mind". The pain shows up as a side detail, often with a number (hours, money), with no complaint and no wish for change. |
| 2. Problem aware | The buyer feels the problem and says so, but does not know any way to fix it. | Names the pain as a problem and shows feeling ("drives me mad", "I hate that part"), then signals no known fix: "I've no idea how to stop it", "is there even a way round that", "I assumed nothing could be done". |
| 3. Solution aware | The buyer knows a type of fix exists but has not connected it to this seller's product. | Names a category or method of fix without naming this product: "I've seen software that does that", "a friend uses an online form for it", "I keep meaning to hire someone for it". Has not picked one, or has not acted. |
| 4. Product aware | The buyer knows this seller's product and is weighing it, but has doubts. | Talks about this product by name, by "your", or by a feature they have seen, and raises a doubt about whether it is right for them. |
| 5. Most aware | The buyer knows the product, wants it, and is asking about terms or next steps. | Asks how, when, or on what terms to buy: "where do I sign", "can we start next week", "send me the paperwork", "what's the setup process". |

## Telling neighbouring stages apart

Ask these questions in order. A "no" to question 1 means Unaware. A "no" to question 2 means Problem aware. A "no" to question 3 means Solution aware. Stop at the first "no". If all three are "yes", question 4 decides between Product aware and Most aware.

1. **Unaware or problem aware?** Does the buyer themselves frame it as a problem they want gone? If they call it normal, fine, or "just how it is", it is unaware, even if the seller later calls it a problem.
2. **Problem aware or solution aware?** Does the buyer name any type of fix, method, or tool category? If not, it is problem aware.
3. **Solution aware or product aware?** Does the buyer connect the fix to this seller's product (by name, by "your", or by a feature they saw)? If not, it is solution aware.
4. **Product aware or most aware?** Is the buyer's open question **whether** to buy (doubt) or **how and when** to buy (terms, start date, setup)? Whether is product aware. How and when is most aware.

## Rules for tagging a transcript

- **Tag what the buyer knew coming into the call, not what the seller taught them during it.** If a buyer says "I've no idea how to stop it", then the seller explains a feature and the buyer says "really, you can do that?", the pain is problem aware. The surprise confirms where they started. What the buyer learned before the call counts: a demo, a trial, a feature they already use. When a buyer refers to something they saw or tried before the call, that is knowledge they brought in, even if the seller mentioned the same thing a minute earlier.
- **Read every buyer line about the pain, not only the line that names it.** The deciding signal often comes a line or two later, such as a question about terms right after the pain is stated.
- **Only the buyer's words count.** A seller saying "a lot of clients struggle with X" is not evidence that this buyer has pain X, and it is never a signal for a stage.
- **One pain point, one stage.** If the same pain appears twice in a call, tag it once, using the buyer's first clear statement of it.
- **Existing customers still have stages.** On a client call, the stage is about the fix for this pain. A paying customer who does not know the product already solves their problem is problem aware or solution aware for that pain, not most aware.

## Where sources disagree, and the choice made here

- **Labels.** Schwartz used descriptions, not names. Later writers use "problem aware"; Copyhackers uses "pain aware". This note uses "problem aware" because it is the most common label.
- **Order.** Schwartz listed most aware first; most later guides list unaware first. This note lists unaware first, because a buyer moves in that direction.
- **Chapter.** Secondary summaries place the passage in chapter 2 or chapter 3 of the book. This note does not cite a chapter.
- **Customer journey models are a different thing.** McKinsey's "consumer decision journey" (Court, Elzinga, Mulder and Vetvik, 2009) maps the steps of a purchase: consider, evaluate, buy, enjoy, advocate, bond. It describes what a buyer does, not what they know. This folder tags what the buyer knows, so it uses Schwartz's stages as the journey.
